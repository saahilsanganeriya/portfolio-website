A client writes in: family of four, two weeks in Japan, spring, nothing too packed. They do not mention
that one of them uses a wheelchair. A good travel agent asks. A bad one books the charming hotel up three
flights of stairs.

travel-agent-gym is an environment for training and testing agents on that whole job. The agent
interviews the client, decides where the trip goes, searches flights, rail and hotels, builds a schedule
that fits a real clock, gets it approved and books it. Then the trip happens. Flights slip and
monsoon ferries cancel, so anything that no longer fits gets missed.

## How it is built

The client's preferences are hidden: eight axes, of which the opening message reveals one to three. The
reward is the trip's value under those hidden weights, so asking questions pays for itself. Nobody has to
tell the agent to do it.

Hard needs work the same way. A constraint the agent has asked about is enforced by the action mask, so
an informed agent cannot fumble it. A constraint it never asked about is not masked. It can book the
inaccessible hotel, and the trip fails when the family arrives.

Illegal actions are removed by the action mask rather than punished. The two obvious exploits, always booking the cheapest flight and always picking the top-rated hotel, both score zero with
no penalty written against either. Cheap fragile fares lose once the disruptions start. Ratings from a
handful of reviews are noisy, and price follows true quality, so sorting by rating overpays. No LLM sits
in the reward path. The reward is arithmetic over a booking ledger.

The first version had a clock bug I am glad I found: it applied Tokyo's time offset to a San Francisco
departure, so every long-haul leg landed at the wrong local time. The second version builds every time from
real time zones and checks the whole world for coherence. On its first run that check caught flights from
San Francisco to San Francisco.

## Where the difficulty went wrong

I wanted a random policy to solve almost none, and my scripted agent to land near 30%, solving some trips
and failing many. Version one missed at both ends. Random solved one episode in eight and finished one in
four, so a trip could be completed by accident. My heuristic solved 75%, and a trained PPO policy 88%.

Version two widened the world to 3 regions, 13 cities and 5 transport modes. Random fell to zero, which
was the fix that mattered. The heuristic fell to 5%, past the other edge. An expert version that asks every
hard-constraint question did no better, at 2%, so the interviewing I built the world around does not yet
show in the score.

The heuristic was not losing on judgement. It finished only 8% of trips. I found and fixed two causes.
Nothing held back money for the rest of the trip, so one outbound flight could take 97% of the budget, and
the heuristic kept repeating the same empty search. The 8% is what is left after those fixes. Raising the
turn cap from 90 to 150 made it worse, because the client's patience ran out first.

Then I trained PPO for 500k steps. It learned to book nothing: its training reward settled at −0.00005,
with episodes running out the 90-turn clock. Doing nothing scored −0.360. Building a real trip, as the
heuristic does, scored −1.318. Stalling beats working in this reward, and PPO found that. A small LLM agent
did the right things, finding 91% of the preference axes and asking about every class of hard constraint,
and still completed no trips. That points at the environment, not the agents.

## What I would change

Fix completion first. At 8% completion no reward shape can save it. Then make walking away the
worst outcome, instead of a floor an agent can retreat to. An environment that is hard because nobody can
finish it is broken, and the only way to tell the two apart is to say which one you measured.
