A client writes in: family of four, Japan in spring, around $4k. They do not say that one of them uses a
wheelchair, or that food matters to them more than anything else on the trip. A good travel agent finds
both out before booking anything. A bad one books the cheapest flight and the best-rated hotel, and the
trip comes apart in the second week.

travel-agent-gym is a reinforcement-learning environment built around that job. The agent interviews the
client, searches, builds an itinerary that has to fit a real clock, books it against a budget, and then the
trip actually happens. The reward is the trip the client got, not the plan.

## How big the job is

The client has eight hidden preferences: cost, comfort, location, pacing, food, culture, novelty and
reliability. The opening message reveals one to three of them. In the second version the party is hidden
too: each traveller's age, passport, access needs and diet. The client loses patience every turn and can
walk out.

The world has 3 regions (Japan, Italy and Thailand), 13 cities and 43 districts. There are 5 ways to get
around, from rail to ferries, on local timetables, and 7 kinds of places to sleep. An episode can last 90
turns, and the agent picks from 89 actions on each one.

Then Act II runs the trip. Delays, cancellations, overbooking, weather and strikes fire on what was
actually booked, and a delay travels down the timeline as far as it really reaches. A cheap fare with a
45-minute connection finally costs what it costs.

## The shortcuts an agent would take

Any agent, human or learned, reaches for the same few shortcuts. I wanted each of them to lose on its own,
without a penalty written against it.

- **Book the cheapest flight.** Cheap and fragile loses in expectation once the delays start.
- **Book the top-rated hotel.** A rating from a handful of reviews is noisy, and price follows true
  quality. Over 200 generated worlds, sorting by rating picks lucky small hotels and overpays.
- **Book without asking.** The weights are hidden, so an unasked booking is a bet on the average client.
- **Ask forever.** Patience runs out and the client leaves.

The first version of the world had a different kind of shortcut: mine. Its clock had three silent
time-zone bugs. One gave every San Francisco departure Tokyo's offset from UTC.

## The design

**Asking pays for itself.** The reward is the trip's value under the client's hidden weights. Nobody tells
the agent to ask questions; it just scores better when it knows what the client wants.

**Hard needs are masked only once asked.** If the agent asks about access needs, the action mask removes
every inaccessible hotel, so an informed agent cannot fumble it. If it never asks, nothing is masked. It
can book the hotel up three flights of stairs, and the trip fails on arrival.

**Illegal is impossible, not punished.** Over budget, the wrong city, a museum closed that day: the mask
removes those actions. The booking ledger counts integer cents, so overspending cannot happen at all.

**One inequality instead of a pile of rules.** Every pair of things on the itinerary has to satisfy
`a.end + transfer(a, b) <= b.start`. That one line catches an over-packed day, a move between cities
with no booked leg, and a tour that starts before the flight lands.

**No LLM in the reward.** It is arithmetic over the ledger, so the same seed always gives the same reward.
The shaping term is a potential difference, which by construction cannot change which policy is best.

The second version builds every time from real time zones, derives arrivals from distance and checks the
whole world for coherence. On its first run the check caught flights from San Francisco to San Francisco.

## What I gave up

The world is synthetic. Travel times between places come from 108 real distance pairs fetched once, and
nothing touches the network during an episode. I traded real inventory for a reward that never changes
under you.

The shaping guarantee cuts both ways. Shaping that cannot change the best policy also cannot rescue a
terminal reward that favours the wrong thing. That is the next section.

## Where the difficulty went wrong

I wanted a random policy to solve almost nothing and my scripted agent to land near 30%: low enough that a
learner has something to find, high enough that it ever sees a success.

Version one missed at both ends. Random solved one episode in eight and finished one in four, so a trip
could be completed by accident. The heuristic solved 75%, and a trained PPO policy 88%.

Version two is the bigger world described above. Random fell to zero, which was the fix that mattered.
The heuristic fell to 5%, far past the target. An expert version that asks every hard-constraint question
did no better, at 2%, so the interviewing the whole world is built around does not yet show in the score.

The heuristic was not losing on judgement. It finished only 8% of trips. Two causes were found and fixed:
nothing held money back for the way home, so one outbound flight could eat 97% of the budget, and the
heuristic kept repeating the same empty search. The 8% is what is left after those fixes. Raising the turn
cap from 90 to 150 made it worse, because the client's patience ran out first.

Then I trained PPO for 500k steps, and it learned to book nothing. Its training reward settled at −0.00005,
with episodes running out the 90-turn clock.

<figcaption>Doing nothing beats every policy that tries to build a trip, which is the reward PPO learned to exploit.</figcaption>

A trip that gets booked absorbs disruption damage. A policy that stalls pays only for the client's lost
patience. PPO found that. A small LLM agent did everything right, finding 91% of the preference axes and
asking about every class of hard constraint, and still completed no trips. When an agent that does
everything right hits the same wall, the wall is the environment.

One more correction, against myself. My own commit messages recorded the version-one heuristic at 96% and
the version-two one at 7%. Re-running the committed code gives 67–83% and 5%. Every number on this page is
the re-run.

## What's next

Completion comes first. At 8% completion no reward shape can save it. Then the terminal reward needs
rebalancing: walking out costs a flat 1.0, while disruption damage can reach 2.0 and is scaled by 0.9.
Walking away should be the worst outcome, not a floor to retreat to.
