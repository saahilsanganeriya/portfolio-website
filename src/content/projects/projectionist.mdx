In an old cinema the projectionist sits in the booth at the back. When the show runs well, nobody in the
audience thinks about them. That is the job I wanted from the software behind my film library.

The library lives on a four-bay Synology NAS with a low-power Celeron. For a long time it ran on Python
scripts, some on a timer and some I started by hand. One renamed files, one fetched subtitles, one picked
posters, and none of them knew what the others were doing.

## What it does

projectionist replaces those scripts, one at a time, with one Go service in one container. Each job is a
stage with its own schedule and, where it matters, a night-time window. State lives in SQLite, and the
service answers `/health` and `/metrics`. There are 15 stages now, covering renaming new arrivals against
TMDB, subtitles, posters and collections, watch history across Plex and Jellyfin, a library audit,
backups of the apps' data, and health checks that can restart a stuck service.

The name is the main rule. Every disruptive job pauses while anyone but me is watching. A gate counts the
Plex and Jellyfin sessions that are not mine. Before it moves a file it waits for zero, and during a long
encode it sends SIGSTOP and SIGCONT. If it cannot read the count, it assumes someone is watching.

Each stage has to be safe to rerun after a crash: it finishes the work and never repeats a side effect.
Every file move is a single system call that cannot overwrite anything, and I tested that call on the NAS
itself before trusting it. A Python script goes away only when its Go stage matches it on a recorded
gate. The rename planner agreed with the old script on 21,333 of 21,334 rows, and the one difference was
intended.

## What broke

**Quick subtitles.** An early mode wrote any subtitle that passed a cheap length check. A spot check found
4 of 5 such files 1.3 to 55 seconds out of sync, and nothing ever revisits a subtitle once it is written.
I deleted the mode. Every subtitle is now fitted to its video first, from 4 windows of 90 s each, and on
the gate every sampled fit sat within 134 ms of a whole-file reference.

**A restart that was half a restart.** Docker's one-call restart killed Jellyfin while it was still
cancelling a scan, then never ran the start half. Restarts are now three steps: stop with a grace period,
start, then poll the service until it answers. No reply from Docker counts as proof.

**A slow Plex.** Mass edits fragmented Plex's database until the Movies page took 26.8 s to load cold.
Plex's own database optimise brought it to 17 ms. The health stage now times those pages on every run and
schedules the optimise itself, at night, while nobody but me is watching.

**An alert that would not clear.** One stale subtitles alert held `/health` at 503 for about 12 hours of a
clean run and emailed me every 15 minutes. Now a run that has gone 30 minutes without raising anything
clears it.

## Small things I like

Posters are picked by a vision model under a hard budget of $12, and it never replaces a poster I chose by
hand. My own picks are often deliberately odd art, which the judge marks down, so it does not get a vote
on them.

My Letterboxd diary flows into the watch history from Letterboxd's public RSS feed and pages, and the
whole service sends Letterboxd at most one request every 2 seconds. The Films page on this site reads the
same feed at the same polite pace.

The repository holds 610 commits and about 62,000 lines of Go across 172 files, 66 of them tests. It
ships as a 12 MB static binary.

## What I would change

Quick mode is the lesson. Anything that writes into the library has to prove itself before it writes,
because nobody checks a file twice.
