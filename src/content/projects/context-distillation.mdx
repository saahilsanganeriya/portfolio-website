Put a few examples in a language model's prompt and it can often do a task it was never trained for. The
examples cost context on every call, though. Context distillation, a method Anthropic described, tries to
bake the effect of a prompt into the weights instead.

For a deep learning course, four of us tested it against plain fine-tuning, pattern-based fine-tuning and
in-context learning, with and without BitFit, which trains only the bias terms. The models were OPT at
125M, 350M and 1.3B parameters, the largest our hardware could hold. Each model saw 16 examples. We
measured accuracy on three inference datasets and on harder out-of-domain sets, over up to 5 random seeds
per setting.

My part was the RTE dataset and its preprocessing, BitFit on RTE, and the plain and pattern-based
fine-tuning runs on every dataset at all three sizes. I also wrote the paper's Approach section.

## What we found

Accuracy sat between 50% and 60%, barely above chance. One dataset looked better until we checked its
labels: with 40% true labels, answering "false" every time scores 60%, and 72% on its out-of-domain
partner. We found no evidence that context distillation helps at this scale. It also trains much more
slowly, because its loss runs over the top 50 logits at every token instead of one label.

## What I would change

We spent our time on careful comparisons at sizes too small to show any difference, and the original
paper's gaps appear in larger models. One or two runs that worked at a large size would have taught us more
than five seeds at three small ones.

The linked repository holds the report and only the RTE scripts for plain and pattern-based fine-tuning.
The code for the other experiments is not in it.
