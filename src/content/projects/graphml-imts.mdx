In an ICU record every variable runs on its own clock. Heart rate is charted all through a stay. A troponin test happens only when a doctor orders one.

The PhysioNet 2012 challenge data has 12,000 ICU stays, the first 48 hours of each, with 36 variables measured over time. Lay that out as a table of variables by time and 85.7% of the slots are empty. For a graph machine learning course in fall 2025, four of us tried to forecast what a patient’s numbers do next.

## Do not fill the gaps

The usual first move is to fill the gaps, then hand a neat table to an ordinary forecaster. With most of the table filled in by us, a model would learn mostly from our guesses. So every model in the project took the sparse record as it is.

I built the shared pipeline that every model read from. It splits the patients the same way for everyone, normalises with training data only, and keeps extreme values, such as a heart rate of 300, because in an ICU they can be real. It also turns each patient into a graph of their measurements.

<figure class="os-figure">

```text
 hour            10:00      11:30      13:15
 heart rate        o----------o----------o
                   |                     |
 blood pressure    o                     |
 troponin                                o

 o  one measurement
 -  same variable, at most 2 hours apart
 |  different variables, same time
```

<figcaption>An observation graph: every measurement is a node, joined to the same variable nearby in time and to other variables measured at the same moment. A real patient has about 150 nodes and 500 edges.</figcaption>
</figure>

My model was RainDrop, a published graph network for irregular sensor data, which learns a second graph of its own between the variables. It was built to classify patients, and I adapted it to forecast: given the first 24 hours, predict hours 24 to 30.

## A number that flatters me

RainDrop reached an RMSE of 0.561 on validation and 0.596 on test. The targets are z-scored, so that is about six-tenths of a standard deviation off. Teammates’ models scored 0.643 (T-PatchGNN) and 0.724 (GRU-D) on test after tuning, so RainDrop looks best.

It is not a fair race. My scoring code compares one mean per patient and variable over those six hours, a much smoother target than the single values the other models predict. The table shares a split and a normalisation, but it does not rank the models. Across all of them, variables measured often, like heart rate, were forecast better than sparse labs, and extreme values came out too small.

## What I would change

I would score every model on the same target before comparing anything. Then I would report errors in clinical units next to the normalised ones. Six-tenths of a standard deviation of heart rate might matter a great deal at the bedside, or not at all.
