An ICU chart is not a neat table. Heart rate might be logged every few minutes, a lab value twice a day,
and some variables never. In the PhysioNet 2012 challenge data, about 86% of the possible measurements
are missing.

For a graph machine learning course, four of us asked whether graph neural networks could forecast a
patient's vitals and labs 6 to 24 hours ahead from 24 hours of history, using the irregular data as it is
instead of filling the gaps first. Each patient becomes a graph. Every measurement is a node, with edges
to the same variable nearby in time and to other variables taken at the same moment.

The data covers 12,000 patients, 48-hour windows and 41 variables. We kept extreme values, such as a heart
rate of 300, instead of throwing them out, since in an ICU they can be real.

My part was the repository and the data exploration, and adapting RainDrop, a published graph model for
irregular sensor data, from classifying patients to forecasting. It reached an RMSE of 0.561 on validation
and 0.596 on test. The targets are z-scored, so that is about six-tenths of a standard deviation off.

## What I would change

That number is on normalised values and says nothing yet about the bedside. Six-tenths of a standard
deviation of heart rate might matter a great deal or not at all. I would report errors in clinical units
next to the normalised ones.
