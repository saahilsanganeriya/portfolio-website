The random forest I trained scored 98.2%. It had learned to recognise our own made-up data, not crashes.

For a machine-learning course in spring 2023, five of us tried to predict whether a crash happens, given a place, a time, and the road, weather, driver and vehicle. We had the public crash reports of Montgomery County, Maryland, 155,707 of them after cleaning. Crash reports only record crashes. To tell a risky moment from a safe one, a model also needs safe moments, and the data has none. Everything after that depends on how you invent them.

## Two ways to invent a non-crash

We dropped every field known only after a crash, such as severity and fault. Then we made two sets of non-crashes. I built the random set with a teammate, and another teammate built the hand-built set.

## What the two numbers meant

The same random forest scored 98.2% with the hand-built negatives and 67.7% with the random ones. Location alone gives most hand-built rows away, so the 98.2% measured how well the model spots our generator.

The 67.7% is the honest number, and it is worse than it looks. With three non-crashes for every crash, a model that always answers “no crash” scores 75.0%. Mine found 651 of the 31,141 real crashes in the test set, 2%. XGBoost on all features found the most, 17%, at 71.6% accuracy. No model in the project beat the always-“no crash” answer on random negatives.

The random set gives up that easy win. Its non-crashes are real records with one detail swapped, so they sit where real crashes sit: 3.8% of each fall outside the box. That is the point of it, and it left us nothing to celebrate.

## What I would change

I would start from how much traffic each road carries at each hour. A busy road at rush hour has more crashes because it has more cars, and a model that cannot tell those apart is measuring traffic, not risk. I would also score every model against the always-“no crash” answer from day one, so a number like 98% has to explain itself before anyone puts it in a headline.
