Crash reports only record crashes. To train a model that tells risky moments from safe ones you also need
safe examples, and the data has none. Everything after that depends on how you invent them.

We used 155,108 crash records from Montgomery County, Maryland, and dropped every field known only after a
crash, such as severity and fault. Then we built two sets of made-up non-crash examples. The random set
took a real crash and changed its road segment, hour or day, at three non-crashes for every crash. The
hand-built set drew each feature from realistic distributions instead.

A random forest scored 98% accuracy with the hand-built negatives and 67.7% with the random ones. The
67.7% is the honest number, and it is worse than it looks. With three non-crashes for every crash, a model
that always answers "no crash" scores 75%. Ours caught 2% of the real crashes. The 98% meant the model had
learned to spot our generator, not crashes.

My part was cleaning the data for all three models, and the introduction and problem definition in our
proposal.

## What I would change

I would start from how much traffic each road carries at each hour. A busy road at rush hour has more
crashes because it has more cars, and a model that cannot tell those apart is measuring traffic, not risk.
