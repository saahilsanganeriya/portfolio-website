A phase-contrast image of kidney cells looks like grey cobblestones pressed together. A biologist who
wants to count them, or measure each one, needs an outline around every cell, and one image can hold
about 300. Segment Anything, Meta's general segmentation model, will outline almost anything you click on.
Clicking 300 times per image is the problem.

## Why the outlines matter

Cell counts and cell sizes are the measurements a lab draws conclusions from. A segmentation that merges
two touching cells into one does not just look wrong. It undercounts the cells and inflates their average
size, and both errors flow straight into the science.

And there is a lot to outline. Cells in culture routinely number in the hundreds per image, so drawing them
by hand is the slow part of the whole experiment.

## The two obvious versions

**Segment Anything as it ships.** Its automatic mode scatters prompt points over a grid and outlines what
each one hits. On densely packed cells it outlines clumps instead of cells, and it happily segments empty
patches of the cover slip. A biologist can fix that by clicking every cell, which is the work we were
trying to remove.

**Fine-tune it to paint masks.** Earlier work adapted Segment Anything to medical images by teaching it to
label each pixel: cell or background, or cell, border and background. On cells with faint edges, that
merges neighbours along the weak boundary. Weighting the loss toward boundary pixels, the usual fix,
did not save it.

## The design: predict distance, not class

The idea is borrowed from Cellpose, which already treats segmentation as regression. Instead of asking
"is this pixel a cell?", SAMCell predicts how far each pixel sits from the edge of its cell, scaled so the
middle of every cell reads 1. Touching cells then stop being one blob: between them the target map dips
to zero, which gives the model a reason to keep them apart even where the edge in the image is faint.

<figure class="os-figure">

```text
           cell A        cell B
  1.0 |        /\            /\
      |       /  \          /  \
      |      /    \        /    \
 0.47 |-----/------\------/------\-----  peak: one seed
      |    /        \    /        \  inside each cell
 0.09 |---/----------\--/----------\---  fill: every pixel
  0.0 |__/            \/            \__  of some cell
                      ^
           where A and B touch, the map
           dips, and watershed splits them
```

<figcaption>A schematic slice through the predicted distance map across two touching cells, with the two thresholds that turn it into outlines.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two thresholds turn the map into cells. A high one, 0.47, leaves one small seed inside each cell. A low
one, 0.09, marks every pixel that belongs to some cell. Watershed, a classic algorithm, then floods out from
each seed until it meets the cell's edge or a neighbour's flood. That last step is what pulls touching
cells apart.

We fine-tuned the image encoder and the mask decoder and froze the prompt encoder, which has nothing left
to do. Training used 5,000+ LIVECell images, with CLAHE contrast enhancement. One small trick helped
generalisation: inverting images at random, because phase contrast shows dark cells on a light background
and dark-field microscopy shows the opposite.

## My part

The paper has seven authors. Alexandra Dunnum VandeLoo and Nathan Malta are joint first authors, and I am
third.
I proposed the distance-map regression with watershed post-processing, trained the model, built both new
test datasets and wrote the napari plugin. An expert biologist among the co-authors annotated those
datasets by hand, cell by cell. The public code lives in my fork of Nathan's repository, where
17 of the 22 commits are mine.

## What it costs

Segment Anything's encoder is large. An image is cut into 256×256 windows that overlap by 32 pixels, and
each window is scaled up to the encoder's 1024×1024 input. That is a lot of compute per image, and a
lighter model with the same distance-map head is an open question the paper lists for future work.

Watershed was a choice too. Simple connected components cannot split touching cells. Marker-controlled
watershed needs more precise markers, and graph cuts need more tuning. Plain watershed with two fixed
thresholds is predictable, at the price of struggling with odd shapes such as dividing cells.

## Results

Every image in LIVECell, the dataset SAMCell trains on, comes from the same microscope. A model can ace its
test set and fall apart on someone else's bench. So I built two new datasets for that question, PBL-HEK and
PBL-N2a: phase-contrast images of two common cell lines, taken on different microscopes, 5 images each with
about 300 cells per image. We used them only for zero-shot testing.

<figcaption>SAMCell against Cellpose, the strongest baseline: on the standard test set, and zero-shot on a microscope and cell line it never saw.</figcaption>
</figure>

On LIVECell, SAMCell beat Cellpose and StarDist. On PBL-HEK it nearly doubled Cellpose's score.

The new datasets are small, 10 images in all. That is enough to show the zero-shot gap, not enough to rank
two close methods. Every method also scored much higher on PBL-N2a, whose round, high-contrast cells are
easier, so a single average across the two would hide where the difficulty is.

A model only helps biologists if they can run it. I shipped
[samcell-napari](https://github.com/saahilsanganeriya/samcell-napari), a plugin for the napari image
viewer: point it at a model, set the two thresholds, and the cells come back as a labels layer. It is on
PyPI, and `pip install "napari[all]" samcell-napari` installs it with napari.

The paper is VandeLoo AD, Malta NJ (joint first authors), Sanganeriya S, Aponte E, van Zyl C, Xu D,
Forest C, *SAMCell: Generalized label-free biological cell segmentation with segment anything*, PLOS ONE,
2025.

## What's next

[DINOCell](https://github.com/saahilsanganeriya/DINOCell) is my follow-up: the same distance-map head on a
DINOv3 self-supervised backbone, pretrained on Cell Painting images streamed from a public bucket. It has
no results yet. I list it because it is where the idea goes next, not because it works.
