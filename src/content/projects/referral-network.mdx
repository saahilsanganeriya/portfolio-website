A refers B. B refers C. Months later a form somewhere records that C referred A. If the system accepts
that, A now sits below A, every count that walks up the chain walks forever, and the question "who
brought in the most people?" has no answer.

A referral program grows like a family tree, and most of the questions about it are about shape. Who
brought in the most people, directly or not? Who sits on the most referral chains? And the one with money
in it: how big a bonus does it take to grow the network to a target size?

referral-network answers those questions in about 200 lines of Python.

## The rules that make it a tree

Each person has exactly one referrer. Nobody can refer themselves, and no referral may close a loop.

<figure class="os-figure">

```text
 add a referral: d refers a?

    a   <-- the walk reaches a, the candidate,
    |       so this referral would close a loop:
    b       refused, with a clear error
    |
    c       the check walks up from the referrer:
    |       d -> c -> b -> a
    d
```

<figcaption>The loop check: walk up from the referrer to the top of its tree, and refuse if the walk meets the person being referred.</figcaption>
</figure>

Those three rules make the network a forest: a set of trees with no loops. Everything below depends on
that.

## Who brought in the most

A person's reach is how many people sit below them, at any depth. The obvious way to rank by reach is to
walk every person's subtree whenever someone asks. That walks the whole network for every question.

Instead the library keeps reach current as people join. A new person adds their own subtree, plus
themselves, to the reach of everyone above them. Asking for the top people then means reading numbers that
are already there.

## Who sits on the most chains

The more interesting measure is flow centrality: how many pairs of people have this person on the
referral chain between them. In a general graph that means finding shortest paths between every pair of
people, which grows with the square of the network at best.

In a forest there is at most one path between two people. A person sits on the path from someone above
them to someone below them, and on no other path. So flow centrality is just the number of people above
times the number of people below. One multiplication replaces a search over every pair.

## How big a bonus

The last part is a small model of growth. Start with 100 referrers, each able to make 10 successful
referrals. Every day each active referrer succeeds with probability p and uses up one referral, and each
new person joins the next day with a full allowance of their own. Rather than simulate people, the library
tracks the expected number of referrers at each remaining allowance, which gives the expected network size
after any number of days in one pass.

On top of that sits a search for the smallest bonus, in $10 steps, that reaches a target size. You pass in
how likely a person is to join at a given bonus. Checking every $10 step would ask that question far too
often, so the search doubles first and then narrows.

<figure class="os-figure">

```text
 1. double until the target is reachable
    $10    $20    $40    $80    $160
    no     no     no     no     yes

 2. binary search from $0 to $160, in $10 steps
    $80 no    $120 yes    $100 no    $110 yes

    answer: $110, the smallest step that reaches it
```

<figcaption>One run of the bonus search, with an adoption curve that first reaches the target at $110; each bonus is asked about once.</figcaption>
</figure>

It gives up if adoption stops rising for 5 doublings in a row, since more money would buy nothing.

## What it costs

Keeping reach current makes every question cheap and every insert dearer. Each new person walks up to the
top of their tree twice, once for the loop check and once to update reach. For a bushy tree that is a few
steps. For one long chain it adds up to quadratic time. I measured it: building a chain of 2,000 people
took 235 ms and a chain of 4,000 took 949 ms, four times as long for twice the people. A bushy tree of
4,000, ten referrals each, took 5 ms.

The rankings are sorted lazily, on the first question after a change. That is cheap when people join in
a batch and then someone asks. It is not cheap when every insert is followed by a question: the same bushy
tree of 4,000 took 3,027 ms when I asked for the top three after every insert.

The growth model gives the expected size and nothing else. It cannot say how likely the network is to fall
well short of that.

## What I'd change

The bonus search trusts that adoption only rises as the bonus rises, and nothing checks that. Its binary
search also starts again from $0, although, if adoption does rise with the bonus, the doubling has
already ruled out everything up to the last bonus that fell short. A chain-heavy network needs a different way to keep reach current.

And the first version had no tests at all, which for code this easy to get subtly wrong was a mistake.
Tests came almost nine months later, when I published it.
