A referral program grows like a family tree. One person brings in three friends, one of those friends
brings in two more, and after a few months the questions that matter are about shape: who brought in the
most people, directly or not, and who sits on the most chains. Then there is money. How big a bonus does
it take to hit a growth target?

referral-network answers those questions in about 200 lines of Python.

## The rules

Each candidate has exactly one referrer. Nobody can refer themselves, and no referral may close a loop. To
check for a loop, the library walks up from the referrer toward the root. If it meets the candidate on the
way, the referral would make a cycle, and it refuses with a clear error.

Those rules make the network a forest: a set of trees. Everything below depends on that.

## Reach and flow

A person's reach is how many people sit below them, at any depth. The library keeps reach current on
every insert by adding the new candidate's subtree size to each ancestor, so asking for the top people
by reach never has to walk the whole network.

The more interesting measure is flow centrality: how many pairs of people have this person on the
referral chain that runs from one to the other. In a general graph that means finding shortest paths between every pair. In a forest there
is at most one path between two people, so a person sits on the path from someone above them to someone
below them, and nowhere else. Flow centrality is just the number of people above times the number of
people below. One multiplication replaces a search over every pair.

## Growth and bonuses

The last part is a small model. Start with 100 referrers, each able to make 10 successful referrals. Every
day each active referrer succeeds with probability p, uses up one of their referrals, and each new person
joins the next day with a full allowance of their own. The library tracks the expected number of
referrers at each remaining allowance, which gives the expected network size after any number of days
without simulating anyone.

On top of that it finds the smallest bonus, in $10 steps, whose adoption rate reaches a target size. It
doubles the bonus until the target is reachable, then binary-searches back down. It gives up if the
adoption rate stops rising for 5 doublings in a row, since more money would buy nothing.

## What I would change

Keeping ancestor counts current walks the new candidate's whole subtree on every insert. That is fine for
thousands of people and quadratic for one long chain. The bonus search also trusts that adoption only rises
with the bonus, and nothing checks it. And the first version shipped with no tests at all, which for code
this easy to get subtly wrong was a mistake.
