I first saw *Oppenheimer* on 70mm film on July 20, 2023, and logged it without a rating. Two days later I watched it again, gave it five stars, and wrote the longest review I have written for any film I rated that high. I went back in August for 70mm IMAX, and once more in April 2024.

The film follows J. Robert Oppenheimer, the physicist who ran the Los Alamos laboratory where the first atomic bomb was built, from his student years through the test of the bomb and the hearing that later took away his security clearance. It runs three hours. The color scenes stay with his point of view; the black-and-white scenes follow Lewis Strauss, the official who later turned against him. This is what I wrote about it, put more plainly.

When artists indulge themselves, they make something messy: a fog of ideas, alive and very imperfect, rising and falling in a shape that feels almost human. Each piece holds more than one meaning at once. The pieces contradict each other and collide. The thing exists as a paradox and thrives on disagreeing with itself. Its layers rest on top of one another like frames of celluloid, and the stack has a plain solidity, with mystery wrapped inside it.

*Oppenheimer* is that kind of film. There is a childlike haphazardness in how it was made. It has no clear objective and no obvious priorities. It does not seem to know what it thinks about its own ideas, and it does not seem to want to. It basks in its own glory and rests in its own shadow, the way people do. That is why it feels alive.

It also feels human for a second reason. It takes on its central character so completely that I half wonder whether it happened by accident. The film sets out to give you a look inside Oppenheimer’s mind, and seems to have slipped and got locked in a room in there. It never leaves. It keeps the audience inside with it.

Here is the strange part. The film does not make you feel that you know the man. If anything, it keeps you at a distance. After three hours with him, I doubt you could hold a conversation with him. What it does instead is make you feel that you are him.

And being him turns out to feel a lot like being anyone. You have no idea who you are. You have not the slightest idea what people see when they look at you, good or bad. You only know your own thoughts, and you are shut inside them. There is a dark cloud in front of your eyes that clears only when you close them.

In that state, clear and confused at once, you are handed a choice. Do you do it? You could be the man who moved the earth. You could play God. You could do something so big and so hard to grasp that everything else looks small beside it: something to tie yourself to, so that you do not drift away into the empty space of a life.

You are afraid of it. Not of the fire itself. What swallows your mind is the thought of the bomb’s destruction swallowing the earth, and even that fear is about the part you played. Perhaps you love the guilt, because the guilt means you mean something. Perhaps what matters to you is not whether what you did was right or wrong, but that it was worthy of the argument. Hero, genius, martyr, or death itself: whichever one they decide you were, at least everyone will remember.

The diary entry for that first screening still has no rating. Every entry after it has five stars.
