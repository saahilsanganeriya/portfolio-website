My surname has two spellings, and both are mine. My résumé says Sanganeriya. My email address says sanganeria, without the y. So did the address of the photography blog I kept as a teenager, and so did the name I signed under my blog posts. On GitHub I am saahilsanganeriya, and on Letterboxd I am saahilsan, which is neither.

Sanganeriya is also the name of the spinning mill my father built. Anyone who knows me links every name in that list to me in a second, and leaves the mill out. A program sees a pile of strings that share some letters, and one of them is a company.

That is the same-person problem. It is easy because any of us can do it for a handful of records without thinking. It is hard because a program has to do it for millions of records with no idea what a person is.

## How big it gets

It is also my day job. At Mercor I built the entity resolution that links every name, email and handle in a company’s data to the real person behind it, so that each person can get [one consistent fake identity](/blog/redaction-is-not-enough). This post is about the problem, not about how our system works inside.

One run of mine resolved 88 million mentions. The honest way to answer “which of these are the same person?” is to compare every mention with every other one.

For 88 million mentions that is nearly four quadrillion comparisons. At a million comparisons a second, that takes about 123 years. At a billion a second, it still takes about 45 days, for one run.

## The two easy answers

The first easy answer is to link only exact matches. It never joins two strangers. It also never learns that J. Lee and Jordan Lee are one person, or that Sanganeriya and Sanganeria are, because the strings differ. It looks clean, with almost no wrong links, and it is resolving strings, not people.

The second easy answer is to link anything that looks close. That finds J. Lee, and it starts joining strangers too. Two John Smiths in one company are two people. A first name on its own links strangers all the time.

Then there are things that look like identity and are not. A shared inbox like support@ is many people. So is a family email address, and a household phone number belongs to everyone in the house. Notification emails arrive from one address under many people’s names. “Same address, same person” is a guess about the world, and it is wrong often enough to hurt.

## One bad link

Linking has a trap built into it. If A is the same person as B, and B is the same person as C, then A is C. That is what “the same person” means. It is also why one wrong link joins two whole groups.

I have watched one noisy match pull strangers into one cluster, and it is the failure I worry about most. So a merge should need more than one piece of evidence. In the system I built, a merge needs agreeing votes, so one bad verdict cannot join two people.

## Which mistake can you live with?

There are only two ways to be wrong. You can split one person into two, or merge two people into one. Every method makes both mistakes. The real question is which one you can live with, and that depends on what the answer is for.

If you are cleaning a mailing list, a split means someone gets two copies of a catalogue, and a merge means someone stops getting it. If you are matching hospital records, a merge can put one patient’s allergies in another patient’s chart. In anonymization, a split leaves a seam: one person with two fake names, which reads a little oddly. A merge puts one person’s words and details on someone else’s fake identity, and once the data has shipped nobody can take that back. So when we are unsure, we split.

Decide which mistake you can live with before you write any code. Every threshold and every rule after that is the same decision made again in small.

## Making it fit

Since comparing everything is out, you group likely matches first and compare only inside the groups. I did not invent the trick. Search engines worked it out in the 1990s: MinHash was published in 1997 and first used at AltaVista to find duplicate web pages. Records that share enough pieces tend to land in the same bucket, and nobody compares the rest.

This costs true matches. Two mentions that never land in the same group can never be linked, however obvious the link would be to a person. Asking for agreeing votes costs some too, because a real match with only one piece of evidence stays split. Both costs land on the side I chose to err on.

## Knowing whether you got it right

Measuring this is its own trap. Put every mention in one giant cluster and you have found every true match. Put every mention alone and you have made no wrong merge. Both answers are useless, and some popular scores rate one of them well. A number that cannot fail tells you nothing.

The numbers I can share went the right way. Matching inside groups, with agreeing votes for every merge, lifted B-cubed F1, a standard score for this kind of clustering, from 0.34 to 0.84. With the eval suite I built alongside, accuracy on our labelled set went from 85% to 95%. Neither says which way the remaining mistakes lean, split or merge, and that is the thing that decides whether anyone gets hurt.

## What I would do first next time

I would build the scoreboard before the matcher. Score the two silly answers first, and trust only a measure that rates both of them badly. Then put in the cases that must come out right: two spellings of one surname that have to link, and a spinning mill that must never join them.
