In March 2019 I wrote a long review of *La La Land* for the blog I kept as a teenager. It opened by sorting films into two kinds. Some you want to watch at home, wrapped in a blanket. Others you want to see on a big screen with a hundred other people, even if it means a road trip to the next town. Romantic films usually land in the first group. I argued that *La La Land* belonged in the second.

By then I had seen it eight times, all of them in the first nine months of 2017. I had also watched a great deal of YouTube about how films are made. The film had brought back an interest in movies that had been fading. Before it, I mostly watched mainstream things, and there was rarely much in them to talk about. Eleven days after the review, I posted two portraits on my photography blog and titled them after its hero: “sebastian, la la land.”

The story, I admitted, is predictable. Girl meets boy. They keep running into each other by accident. They dance above Los Angeles at dusk, pretending not to like each other, and then they fall in love. What makes it feel new is the take on the story, not the story. Damien Chazelle had already made *Whiplash*, about one person’s struggle for greatness, in which the hero ends his relationship early so nothing stands in the way. *La La Land* asks what happens if you try to keep both the love and the ambition. Its answer is that you have to let something go, and that a sad ending is sometimes all right, because you chose it.

Most of the review was about details. The close-ups hold just long enough for you to read a face and feel what the character feels. A small hook early on: the two of them at a screening of *Rebel Without a Cause*, hands creeping toward each other in the dark. The colors start loud and primary, almost fake, the way Hollywood looks from outside, and soften into plainer pastels as the film turns honest.

Then the epilogue. Seven minutes of what-if, a life in which everything works out. I called it beautiful and wrong. In that version Sebastian gives up his own club and his own life to follow Mia, and that is not how life works. The film’s real ending, where each of them got the thing they wanted most and lost the other, was to me a happy one.

What the 2019 review did not quite explain is why any of that needs a theatre. The answer was in a paragraph near the end. The first time I saw it, in a cinema, I did not think it was anything special. I enjoyed the songs and the color and did not feel attached to the characters. Then it ended, they were not together, and I found I was much sadder than I expected. I had been attached the whole time without noticing.

I wrote that art should catch you even when you are only half watching. *La La Land* caught me that way, in a cinema, where nobody can pause it.

I still seek out the second kind. Since 2023 I have logged twelve screenings on 70mm film. *Babylon* on 70mm, on New Year’s Day 2023, was one of the only out-of-body experiences I have had at the movies. I saw *Oppenheimer* on 70mm and came back for it in 70mm IMAX. In July and September 2026 I saw *The Odyssey* three times, all in 70mm IMAX.

The first kind has its place, and I have built for it too. [projectionist](/projects/projectionist), one of the projects on this site, is a service that runs on a small server at home. It keeps a film library in order and pauses its heavy jobs whenever someone else is watching. The 2019 review never said one kind of film was better than the other. They need different rooms.

I still give *La La Land* five stars. Four years after I called one scene “a sucker punch in the gut,” I logged it on Letterboxd with one line: sucker punch straight to the heart. That was February 2023. In late February 2024 I watched it again and noticed it was the second year running that I had watched it at the end of February. Maybe, I wrote, this should be a tradition.

It did not become one. I watched it again a month later, then in September 2024, and most recently in August 2026. None of them in February.
