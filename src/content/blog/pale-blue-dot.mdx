On 5 November 2018 I posted three pictures. The first was not mine. It was a band of brown light across black, and about halfway down the band, a speck. The other two were. Rain on a car windscreen at dusk, with the street lamps and the brake lights ahead blurred into soft round dots.

The speck is Earth. My caption opened like a museum label: the date, 14 February 1990, the photographer, Voyager 1, and the distance, about six billion kilometres. Then a note on where to look, the bluish-white speck about halfway down the brown band on the right.

## What the picture is

In February 1990 Voyager 1 was about 40 times as far from the Sun as Earth is, heading out of the solar system. About 34 minutes before it switched its cameras off, it turned around and took a family portrait of the planets, 60 frames in all. Earth's picture was built from three exposures through violet, blue and green filters, and the whole planet covers 0.12 of a single pixel.

The brown bands are not out in space. From where Voyager was, Earth sat only a few degrees from the Sun, and the bands are sunlight scattered by pointing the camera that close to it. Earth happens to lie in one of those rays.

The picture exists because Carl Sagan wanted it. He came up with the idea of turning Voyager's cameras back toward the distant Earth. In 1994 he named a book after the frame, *Pale Blue Dot*, and wrote a passage about it that begins "Look again at that dot." The passage goes on to place everyone who ever lived on that speck. For the picture's 30th anniversary in 2020, NASA put out a new version, processed again with modern software.

## What I wrote under it

My caption did what Sagan's passage does, in fewer words. Everyone who ever existed, and everyone still to come, starts and ends on that dot. So do every loss and every achievement, all the pain and all the happiness.

The argument came near the end. Being that small, I wrote, makes people turn away, because turning away is easy. It is comfortable. The caption closed on two short sentences: "I'm not afraid. I'm fascinated."

That was not a one-off. In March of that year I had posted the second law of thermodynamics, that the entropy of the universe only ever increases, and added that I thought this was beautiful. In October I posted a verse by Richard Feynman about being a universe of atoms, and an atom in the universe.

## Why it hangs in my room

The picture is on the wall of my room as a print, near a framed copy of the Voyager Golden Record. The record rides on the same spacecraft. Voyager 1 left Earth carrying it in September 1977, and more than twelve years later it turned back and took this picture.

The print is NASA's image (credit NASA/JPL-Caltech), which is not under copyright in the United States. The record has [a window of its own](/os#voyager), and the last two sentences of my caption are on a sticky note on the desk.

Voyager 1 kept going. In August 2012 it became the first spacecraft to leave the heliosphere, the bubble the Sun's wind blows around the planets. In August 2024 NASA put it at 164.7 times the distance from Earth to the Sun.
