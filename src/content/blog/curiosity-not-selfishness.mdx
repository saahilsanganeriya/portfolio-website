In January 2019 I posted an essay on a small blog called *The Philosophy of Physics*. It was titled “Physicists are Selfish.” I first wrote about this at sixteen, before I had studied any physics formally. This is the second attempt.

Most of that post was a list of questions. What is reality? Where did the universe come from? If everything could be calculated exactly, would we have free will? Why do we move through time in one direction when we can walk through space in any? Why is there a speed limit, and why does light hold it? I said I could fill three pages with questions like these without trying.

The argument went like this. Driven physicists look like saints. They are not chasing money or fame. They are chasing knowledge that might make life better for everyone. Look closer, I wrote, and you find people serving themselves. Physics may turn out to be good for society, but goodwill is not why people study it. They study it because they want answers to the biggest, least answerable questions there are, and physics is the most rigorous road toward them. I liked the irony: the most logical path in life, taken for the least logical reasons.

The post then asked why anyone would sign up for that. We want a theory of everything, something definite with no loose ends. I thought a theory of everything sounded too simple to be true, and that even if one existed we were nowhere near it. So why spend a life on questions you expect to die without answering?

My answer was that there is no better alternative. The other option is to think about the questions all day and end up with nothing. That seemed to me like living under a cloud of uncertainty and pretending it wasn’t there. When there is a crisis, you try to solve it. You set every other worry aside, break the one big problem into smaller pieces and solve them one at a time. The post called this reductionism and said it was the bedrock of physics. It ended on a question: if we don’t even try, isn’t it all a waste?

I still agree with the shape of that. The motive is personal, and whatever good comes to everyone else comes as a side effect. What I would change is the word in the title.

Selfish means taking something at someone else’s cost. Wanting to know where the universe came from costs nobody anything. If I find out why light has a speed limit, there is not less of that answer left for you. The sixteen-year-old knew the better word. It is in the third paragraph of his post: humans are curious, he wrote, and we really want answers to these questions. Then he put the harsher word in the title.

The blog said so too, if I had listened. Its tagline was “The what and the why.” The second post opened by admitting that I lived in a constant state of existential crisis, and that it was fuel: I wanted to know what the world was made of, and I wanted the whys even more.

Curiosity is the honest name for it, a want strong enough to spend a career on. The physicists in the old post are not selfish. They are curious, and stubborn enough to keep failing at it.

Seven weeks after that post I wrote one more, about relativity. To explain it I reached for a detective. Your partner has been killed in a crowded public place, and you want to know what happened. You drop your assumptions. You take a statement from everyone who was there. You set aside the details that change from one account to the next and keep what stays the same in all of them. That, I said, is relativity as a way of thinking: what changes when you change your point of view, and what stays the same no matter how you look?

I promised special and general relativity next. The blog stopped there, two posts long.

I did not become a physicist. I studied computer science at Georgia Tech. The questions I work on now are smaller than the ones on that list, and some of them have answers. But both of the old posts describe my job.

At work I build systems that decide whether two records describe the same person. Asked of every pair of records in a large dataset at once, that question is too big to answer. So the work starts by grouping likely matches into small blocks, and then settles each block on its own. It is the big problem broken into pieces small enough to solve. It is also the detective’s method: take every account and keep what holds.

None of the questions on the 2019 list has been answered since I wrote it. The blog is still online, two posts long, waiting on special relativity.
